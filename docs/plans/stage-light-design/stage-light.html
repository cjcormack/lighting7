Pools, cloth and focus

The Robe 575 on ADV1, aimed at the black backcloth, draws thin stripes at pan 40 and a pool split by a straight seam at pan 45. This report sets out how a beam on a real stage behaves in each of the three areas you raised, what the Stage view does instead and why, and the options for closing the gap.

1 · Consistency

The same beam, five degrees apart

How it should behave

  • A pool is a continuous function of where the head points. Pan it 5° and the pool slides 5° across the cloth; how the cloth takes the light does not change.
  • Whether a point is lit depends only on what lies between it and the lamp. It has nothing to do with where the beam's centre happens to land.
  • On pleated cloth, every fold face turned towards the lamp is lit along its whole width. Faces turned away fall dark, and with raking light a crest throws a shadow into the trough beside it. The banding grows stronger as the light gets more oblique, and it stays continuous as the light moves.

What the Stage view does

There are no shadows. Instead each light carries up to two landing planes (scene/landing.ts): the plane of the face its axis hit (beamReach), and, for a beam split across a convex edge, a second face found by edgeLanding. The surface shader skips a fragment that lies more than REACH_EPS (3 cm) behind both planes. That works for a flat wall or a deck. It fails wherever the drawn surface lies further behind its collider's face than 3 cm.

Why pan 40 is stripes

The drape's collider is a box 10 cm deep (partBox, pleat: half-depth 0.05 m). The drawn cloth is a sine ripple only 5 cm peak to peak (PLEAT_DEPTH_M), centred in that box. So the landing plane, the box's front face, sits 2.5 cm in front of the nearest crest. With a 3 cm skin, the only cloth that passes is the 0.5 cm cap of each crest. That is 20.5 % of every 14 cm pleat, and the facing term thins it further. The rest of the pool is culled as if it were behind the cloth.

Why pan 45 has a seam

A straight vertical seam through the pool has to come from a plane with a level normal. The second landing plane is the only thing that can draw one. edgeLanding casts eight rays round the field's rim. When one lands behind the first face's plane, and the first face lies behind the face that ray hit, that face becomes the second plane. Fragments are culled only when they lie behind both planes. So on the side of the seam that is in front of the second plane, every fragment is lit and the cloth shows whole, the uniform half of your screenshot. On the other side the crest-only stripes return. The candidate faces near a backcloth are the end of the cloth's box, the inner edge of a pair of tabs, or a leg. Whether any of the eight rays clips one of them changes between two pan values with nothing in between, so the seam switches on rather than sliding in.

The same failure elsewhere

  • Columns and lamps. An OBJECT cylinder's collider is a box as wide as its diameter. A beam landing on a column lights a 3 cm sliver at the tangent line, and the rest of its lit side falls dark.
  • The haze reads the same planes (aBeamLand), so the beam in the air can jump at the same pan values as the pool.
  • The drape's own depth is ignored. buildDrape documents depthM as the cloth's fullness, but the mesh uses a fixed 5 cm and the collider a fixed 10 cm.
Two ways to confirm before fixing
  1. Set the pleat collider's half-depth to 0.025 and REACH_EPS_M to 0.06. If the pan 40 stripes become a whole pool, the stripes are the collider and skin mismatch.
  2. Log edgeHit from the director for that head at pan 40 and 45. Expect null at 40 and a level-normal face at 45.

2 · Reflection

What black cloth does under a spot

How it should look

A surface's brightness under a beam is its illuminance times its reflectance, over π. Theatre finishes span two orders of magnitude, and that range is most of what makes a stage read. Under the same beam a white cyc is roughly twenty times brighter than black serge. An eye adapted to a dark house still reads the serge pool clearly. It reads dim, it keeps the beam's colour, and it shows the cloth's texture.

  • Folds. Front light barely shows a drape's folds. Raking light, which a 40° pan across a backcloth is, makes strong rhythmic bands: the face towards the lamp lit, the far face dark, and a crest's shadow falling into the next trough. Stage drapes are hung at 50–100 % fullness, so the folds are deep, and never perfectly regular.
  • Pile. Velour and serge have a soft sheen when the light or the view grazes the surface, from the fibres standing up. There is no mirror highlight.
  • A hard edge on folds zig-zags, because the cloth's surface steps back and forth in depth as the edge crosses it.
  • Floors are glossy. A black dance floor or a painted deck has a satin specular: from the house you see a smeared reflection of the lamp and of bright pools in the floor. On a real stage it is the strongest single cue that a floor is a floor.
  • Light bounces. A bright pool on a pale floor faintly lights the legs and the people near it.

What the Stage view does

Per fragment the surface shader sums every light as colour × mask × (n·L) × spread / falloff and rolls the total off once:

lit = 1 − exp(−(albedo·(ambient + fill) + max(albedo, 0.1)·light) · 1.1)

  • The 10 % floor flattens every dark finish to one grey. #111111 is 0.0056 linear; under light it reflects 0.10, an 18× gain, which is the reflectance of 35 % sRGB grey paint (#595959). The floor is per channel, so it also strips colour. The default drape red #3b1219 is (0.044, 0.006, 0.010) linear; floored it is (0.10, 0.10, 0.10), so a white pool on a red masking drape lands neutral grey.
  • Lambert only. The facing term n·L is the whole BRDF: no sheen on cloth, no gloss on floors, the same response for velour, paint and timber.
  • Pleats never shadow each other. A fold face turned slightly towards the light is lit even where a crest stands between it and the lamp. The ripple is 5 cm deep over a 14 cm pitch, about 1.25× fullness, and a perfect sine with no variation.
  • No bounce, no texture. A pool is a clean vector disc on whatever it lands on, apart from the panels, tiles and boards patterns.
  • Hue shifts at high levels. The roll-off is per channel, so a bright saturated orange runs towards yellow. This is minor at the levels a backcloth sees.

The floor was added so that black serge would show a spot at all. That was a fair problem. The trouble is that it fixes visibility by changing the material rather than the exposure, so materials stop reading differently from one another.

Bloom is out of scope. The renderer deliberately has no post-processing because the composer's targets cost hundreds of MB on Safari (stage-vis-engineering.md §"No post-processing"), and nothing here needs it.

Recommendation. Fix §1 first, because it is most of what looks wrong at pan 40. Then R1 and R2 together, since both retune how cloth reads, then R3 for floors and cloth. R4 and R5 are optional polish.

3 · Focus

What focus does to a pool

How focus behaves

  • A profile or a spot mover images its gate. The field stop, iris, shutters and gobo all sit in or near one plane, and the lens forms an image of it at the focal distance. That image plane is close to flat and square to the beam's axis, so a wall square to the beam is sharp right across the field, not only at the centre.
  • Off that plane each point blurs into a disc. The disc grows with the error in dioptres, |1/d − 1/f|. Light is conserved: the blur spreads the edge both ways, so the half-brightness point stays on the geometric edge. A soft pool is the same size with a feathered rim. Far out of focus it becomes a larger, dimmer blob.
  • Gobos and the field edge share the gate, so they go soft together and by the same amount.
  • A surface at an angle can only be sharp along one band. On a raked backcloth one side of the pool is sharp and the other soft. That much of your pan 45 screenshot is right.
  • Profiles often fringe. Either side of sharp focus the edge picks up a faint blue or amber halo from chromatic aberration, which is how many people find focus at the barrel.
  • Not every "focus" is imaging. On a Fresnel or a PC, focus is spot to flood: beam angle and peak, never sharpness. A PAR has none; a wash mover's is its zoom.

What the Stage view does

The focal-distance side is in good shape: a declared range maps DMX linearly in 1/distance, and Focus here solves it on the desk. The problems are in how a blur is drawn.

  • Defocus pulls the edge inward. beamMask rolls the edge off from 1 − w to 1. Widening w moves the half-brightness point in: to 0.96 of the radius at blur 0.1, 0.87 at 0.25 and 0.725 at 0.55. The soft-centre term then dims the rim by up to another 35 %. A defocused pool shrinks and looks lopsided where a real one keeps its size.
  • Nothing may spill outside the field. The surface shader skips a fragment outside the cone (c < axis.w), and the haze's hull is the cone, so an outward penumbra could not be drawn even if the mask produced one.
  • The blur stops at 0.55 field radii. Hardness reaches zero at FOCUS_SOFT_BLUR and nothing changes after that. A lantern focused at 20 m throwing onto a surface 3 m away looks only a little softer than one 2 m off.
  • Focus is measured along the slant. The surface shader uses dist − aperture.x, the distance from the apex to the fragment, and the haze uses relLen − near, also a slant. Focus here measures along the axis. Focused exactly on a wall square to the beam, the edge, which is the one place sharpness shows, is still out by a factor of 1/cos of the half-angle.
  • Gobos blur through the mip chain. The level is log2(1 + blur × 64) on 128 px tiles with box-filtered mips. From level 3 the pattern comes from 16 px or smaller tiles, magnified bilinearly, so it smears into blocks and diamonds rather than a round blur, and trilinear blending between levels pulses as focus moves.
  • The blur is exaggerated, on purpose. A real profile's lens is 10–15 cm across, and over a 24 m throw that gives a depth of field of metres. A thin lens focused at 21 m blurs a 24 m wall by about 0.002 field radii, less than a pixel. The view's dof was tuned so that a focus move reads, which is a fair choice for a desk. Its shape away from focus also differs from a lens's: in front of focus it is capped at dof field radii where a lens's grows without limit, and behind focus it grows linearly where a lens's levels off.

  • Today the beam bulges at the focal plane. Away from focus the edge is pulled in to 0.725 of the cone, so the beam looks up to 28 % thinner either side of focus and fattest at it, a waist in reverse. A real beam widens steadily along its throw; focus only changes how crisp its edge and its gobo are.
  • Today's silhouette is always the cone. Nothing outside the field is drawn, so a defocused beam never spills, and the pool on the wall shrinks rather than softens.
  • In the other two rows the gobo's shafts converge to sharp lines at the focal plane and blur either side, and the beam's outline feathers outward. The proposed row approximates the lens row closely at a fraction of its cost.

All three rows share the same simplified haze, falloff and blur amount, so the only differences are how a blur is drawn and where it is measured from. The gobo is blurred smoothly in every row: today's mip-chain blocks (F4) are not shown. Blur is clamped at 1.2 field radii in this drawing.

Options

OptionWhat changesWhat it buysCost and riskEffort
F1 · Centre the penumbra on the edge Roll focus blur off from 1 − b to 1 + b (with the hard edge's minimum), widen the cone bound in the light row and the haze hull by the blur, and keep the soft-centre term for frost and family softness only. A soft pool keeps its size and shape. An oblique pool feathers on its far side instead of caving in. Pool and haze stay in step through the shared beamMask. The light's cosBound becomes the field plus its worst blur, computed on the CPU from the focal and landing distances. The 24 m Revolution check needs repeating. S–M
F2 · Measure focus along the axis axial − aperture.x in the surface shader and relLen · cosAngle − near in the haze. Focused on a wall, the whole edge is sharp. Agrees with what Focus here solves. One line in each shader. Slightly changes the tuned look on wide fields. S
F3 · Blur in dioptres b = K · |1/d − 1/f|, with K per family in metres standing for aperture over field, clamped at a maximum. The right shape on both sides of focus. With the 1/d DMX mapping, one DMX step off focus is the same blur anywhere on the fader. A real lens's depth of field shrinks up close. A fixed K exaggerated enough to read at 24 m (72) blurs a 3.5 m surface by 2.6 field radii when the head is focused at 4 m. Keeping today's look means scaling K with f, which gives back today's model near focus. This trades one compromise for another. M
F4 · Filter the gobo blur properly Build the atlas mips with a Gaussian or Kaiser filter at load rather than a box. Optionally take 6–8 disc taps at a lower level for a round blur. No blocks or pulsing; a defocused gobo looks optical. The mip rebuild is a one-off with no runtime cost. The taps cost per lit gobo fragment. S / M
F5 · Chromatic fringe Tint the penumbra slightly blue or amber by the sign of f − d, for imaging families only. Focusing reads like it does at the barrel. A flourish. Easy to overdo. S

Recommendation. F1 and F2 together: they are what looks weird, and both live in code the pool and the haze share. F4 is cheap and worth doing at the same time. I would keep today's relative-error blur amount and leave F3 alone: the amount is a readability choice, and how a blur is drawn is where the view goes wrong. F5 is optional.

Plan

Proposed order

  1. The visible bugs. Option A (drape collider, skin and depthM), F2 and F1. Small, and they remove the worst of all three symptoms. Add the pan-sweep harness and a test pinning the soft edge's half-brightness point at the field radius.
  2. Materials. R1 and R2 together, then retune the pool, haze and fill balance once.
  3. Occlusion. Option C, retiring the landing planes and edgeLanding for surfaces; the haze follows when it can afford it.
  4. Polish. R3 and F4.
  5. Later or never. D (shadow maps), R4 (bounce), R5, F5, and F3 (blur in dioptres).

The design record's constants are judged by eye (D15), and steps 2 and 4 move them. Each needs the same kind of tuning scene the focus work used: a drape under raking light, a floor seen from the house, a wide profile focused on a wall.
