Foreword

Five asks

The desk already knows where every lantern hangs. What it cannot show is the room they hang in, how the stage looks from a seat, or which lantern is which. This book collects the pictures made while working out how it could.

Chris asked for five things. The 3D view should model the real hall, with Claude doing the modelling. You should be able to see the stage from anywhere, a seat in the stalls included. The 2D plan should be fixed or dropped. Fixtures should look like the fixtures, with more choice for the generic dimmers and without glowing balls. And scenery, from tabs to a single prop, should be placeable and move with the show.

Every render in this book comes from the throwaway prototype beside the design record (docs/plans/stage-view-design/). The rig is the real patch of project 15. The hall, the set and the lantern models are estimates, made from a five-second phone video shot from the balcony.

● The room

From a void to a hall

Today the desk can store a stage box, rectangular platforms, straight bars and fixture positions. The hall itself is missing: walls, proscenium, masking, seating, balcony, set. The rig floats in a grid.

Claude builds the room

The venue is a document of named elements, and there are seven kinds: room, proscenium, flat, drape, platform, seating and object. Claude writes it with one set_scene call. It can also fill a proscenium-hall template with a dozen measurements taken from a photo.

A render_view tool would give Claude a picture back from a desk window, so it can compare its model with the photo and correct it.

● Vantage points

Sit anywhere

Viewpoints are saved per project, and every seat is one. A seat view puts your eye 1.15 m above the seat and lets you turn your head; it does not orbit. Pick Sit in a seat, click one, and you are there.

Plan, front and side are cameras, not a second renderer

Today Plan, Front and Side swap the 3D scene for an SVG drawing. Instead, each becomes an orthographic camera on the same scene. It cuts away whatever sits between it and the section plane: the ceilings in plan, the balcony in front, the stage-left wall in side.

● The lanterns

Glass, not lamps

Where the light comes from

Today the desk starts every beam as a point at the lens (FixtureModel.tsx:759). Here a beam leaves its aperture — the lens, or an LED cell's face — at the aperture's own size and shape, and spreads at the field angle. Its apex sits behind the lens by the aperture's radius over the tangent of half the field. For a lantern that is the lamp; for a wide LED face it is far behind, so the beam leaves as a column. One rule covers both.

Aiming does not change: FixtureAim still solves from the placement point, the pivot of the yoke.

The lantern library

A generic dimmer picks a lantern, and so does each of its extra placements, so a pair on one dimmer can be a profile and a fresnel. The library ships with the desk, from datasheets we collect ourselves: GDTF-Share's models cannot be redistributed, and the Open Fixture Library has no tungsten.

PAR beams are ovals of about 2:1, and turning the lamp in the can turns the oval. The patch gains a lamp rotation, and the renderer draws the cone as an ellipse. GDTF has no way to say this.

Shaping the light

A profile has four shutters, a rotating gate and often an iris. A fresnel has barn doors. Every conventional has a focus knob, from a hard edge to a soft one, and some have zoom. Those are set with a spanner at the focus call, so they belong to the lantern's placement rather than to a look. Each lantern of a pair is focused separately.

The focus card

Select a lantern and press Focus. For each shutter or barn door you set a depth and an angle, named for the edge of the light it cuts rather than its place in the gate, where the image is upside down. Then set the gate rotation, the iris, sharp to soft, and zoom within the lantern's range.

Moving lights already render zoom and focus from their channels. The Revolution's iris channel exists but is not drawn today; here it is. A fixture with DMX framing shutters would drive the same four blades from its channels.

Standing, not hanging

The rigging's kind decides the mount. Bars, pipes and trusses are hung from. A ledge or a floor stand is stood on: base down, yoke up, no hanger, and a mover's rest beam points up. A standing body is the hung one turned over.

This matters for aiming too. The desk solves a standing mover differently from a hung one, and the balcony Revolutions are stored as hung.

● Scenery

The set stays where you put it

A tab track is not a light, so scenery changes are their own record, attached to a cue, to a cue stack (its set), or to a Look. Lighting cues on this desk are complete states. Scenery tracks: its state at any cue is every change from the top of the list down to it. GO TO Q3 lands the set as if the list had been run.

Why not a virtual fixture

Everything in the cue pipeline ends as a DMX channel write. A scenery "fixture" would need real channels, and every property category misbehaves for it: some snap halfway through a crossfade, some die at Blackout, some are swept up by a Beam-masked Record. And because cues do not track, a tab closed in cue 14 would open again at cue 15.

Unless it really is one

A motorised tab track on a DMX relay is a fixture, and it is patched as one. The tabs element then follows its channel instead of scenery changes.

● One-shot effects

The party trick

The Twin Shot is a confetti cannon: two tubes, two fire channels, and a master enable. Every fire spends a cartridge, so a fire is an event, not a level. It happens once, on a GO or a held button, only while the desk is armed, and never because a look happened to contain it.

A trigger is not a slider

Today the two fire channels are ordinary sliders, so a Look, a cue or a Beam-masked Record can hold a raised one, which then fires on every recall. The proposal makes them triggers: never a look row, a template, an effect target or a Record capture, and never restored from a channel snapshot.

Armed, and said so

Arming is desk-wide, operator-only, shown on every window, and lapses on its own. A cue event on an unarmed desk is skipped and announced, never queued. In Blind a fire is rehearsed: confetti in the visualiser, nothing on the wire.

Positions

The replacement for the overview panel. There is one row per position, upstage first, with the stage edge marked. Units sit in rig order as seen from the desk, each with its live colour and level. Paired lanterns share a chip. The sheet is derived from the rigging and never stored, so there is nothing to keep in step.

The scaled plan survives as the Plan camera (plate 9), with beams and scenery in it.

● What changes

The running order

  1. 0

    Stop the leaks

    The bloom pass's 8× multisampled buffers, instance buffers sized for the worst case, a React root and a backdrop blur per label, and no recovery when Safari takes the graphics context back. Also the platform drawn 0.95 m proud of the deck, and the lens that glows at zero.

  2. 1

    Cameras and Positions

    Saved and seat viewpoints, orthographic sections on the 3D scene, and Positions in place of the overview panel.

  3. 2

    The scene document

    Elements and viewpoints as portable, synced records, written by set_scene and read by get_scene, with a stage summary in describe_rig.

  4. 3

    Light on every surface

    One shader lights every venue and set surface from the live lights, replacing the per-region cookie instances.

  5. 4

    Claude's eyes

    render_view, rendered by a signed-in desk window.

  6. 5

    Retire the SVG plan

    Editing in the ortho cameras reaches parity, then 3,000 lines go.

  7. 6

    Fixture bodies

    Dark glass, black housings, parametric lantern shapes instanced per part, beams that leave the aperture, multi-head fixtures lit cell by cell, and shutters, barn doors, iris, focus and zoom.

  8. 7

    The lantern library

    Lantern type, zoom and lamp rotation on patches and placements, a body on every DMX type, and oval PAR beams.

  9. 8

    Scenery that moves

    Changes on cues, stacks and Looks, resolved on the desk, streamed as scenery.state, previewed by Next GO.

  10. 9

    The party trick

    Triggers as their own kind of property, cue events, a desk-wide arm, loaded and spent tubes, and confetti in every window. In Blind, a fire is rehearsed.
